# Cohesion Craft Skill

Cohesion is the glue that holds prose together. Without it, readers work too hard to follow. With too much, prose feels mechanical. The art is invisible connection.

## Two Types of Connection

### Cohesion (Text Level)
The explicit linguistic links between sentences:
- Pronouns referring back
- Repeated words
- Connectives (however, therefore, meanwhile)
- Parallel structures

### Coherence (Reader Level)
The logical and conceptual connections the reader builds:
- Cause and effect understanding
- Temporal sequence tracking
- Thematic threading
- Inference and implication

**Goal**: Use enough cohesion that coherence emerges naturally, but not so much that prose becomes mechanical.

## Referential Cohesion

### Pronouns and Antecedents

**The Rule**: Every pronoun must have a clear, unambiguous antecedent.

**Ambiguous**:
"John told Mike that he was wrong."
(Who was wrong?)

**Clear**:
"John told Mike the plan had failed."
or
"John was wrong, and he told Mike so."

### Referential Chains

Build chains of reference through a passage:

```
Sarah entered. → She looked around. → The woman frowned. → Her hand went to her pocket.
```

**Principle**: Alternate between full reference (Sarah, the woman) and reduced reference (she, her) to maintain clarity without monotony.

### Given-New Structure

Each sentence should:
1. Start with something known (given information)
2. End with something new (new information)

**Weak**:
"A large dog was in the yard. Loudly, it barked."
(Starts with new info)

**Stronger**:
"A large dog was in the yard. It barked loudly."
(Given → New)

**Even stronger**:
"A large dog was in the yard. The beast barked like it wanted to tear out my throat."
(Given → More interesting new)

## Lexical Cohesion

### Repetition

Strategic word repetition creates emphasis and connection.

**Key term repetition**:
"The revolution began in March. By April, the revolution had spread to three provinces. No one had expected the revolution to move so fast."

**Danger**: Too much repetition becomes monotonous. Balance with synonyms and pronouns.

### Synonyms and Near-Synonyms

Create variety while maintaining reference:
- doctor → physician → the man in the white coat → Dr. Singh
- house → home → dwelling → the building → the old Victorian

### Collocations

Use words that naturally go together:
- strong coffee (not powerful coffee)
- heavy rain (not weighty rain)
- make a decision (not do a decision)

This creates unconscious smoothness.

### Semantic Fields

Keep related words in the same conceptual neighborhood.

**War passage**: battle, troops, advance, retreat, casualties, victory
**Medical passage**: symptom, diagnosis, treatment, recovery, prognosis

Mixing semantic fields without reason creates dissonance.

## Connectives

### Additive Connectives
Add information, show similarity:
- and, also, moreover, furthermore, in addition
- similarly, likewise, in the same way

### Adversative Connectives
Show contrast or concession:
- but, however, nevertheless, yet, still
- although, though, even though, despite
- on the other hand, conversely

### Causal Connectives
Show cause and effect:
- because, since, as, for
- therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently
- as a result, for this reason

### Temporal Connectives
Show time relationships:
- then, next, afterward, subsequently
- before, previously, earlier
- meanwhile, at the same time, simultaneously
- finally, eventually, ultimately

### Connective Density Guide

| Density | Effect | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Very Low (<1/100 words) | Choppy, disconnected | Never (unless intentional fragmentation) |
| Low (1-2/100 words) | Taut, fast-paced | Action, dialogue-heavy scenes |
| Medium (2-4/100 words) | Balanced, natural | Most prose |
| High (4-6/100 words) | Logical, careful | Argumentation, explanation |
| Very High (>6/100 words) | Mechanical, heavy | Avoid |

## Paragraph-Level Cohesion

### Topic Strings

Each paragraph should maintain a clear topic:

**Paragraph 1**: Topic = the house
"The house stood alone on the hill. Its windows were dark. No one had lived there for years. The garden had grown wild around it."

**Topic string**: house → Its → there → it

### Transition Sentences

Link paragraphs through:

1. **Echo**: Repeat a key word from previous paragraph
2. **Hook**: Use a word that links to the next paragraph
3. **Summary-to-new**: Summarize previous, then pivot

**Example**:
"[End of paragraph about the problem]...and that was why the system was failing.

The solution, when it came, surprised everyone. [New paragraph about the solution]"

### Paragraph Structure for Cohesion

**Known → New throughout**:
- Sentence 1: Introduces topic (given context → new topic)
- Sentence 2: Develops (topic is now given → new detail)
- Sentence 3: Develops further (previous detail → new detail)
- Sentence 4: Concludes or transitions (accumulated given → new implication)

## Causal Cohesion

### Making Causation Clear

Readers need to understand why things happen.

**Unclear causation**:
"The market crashed. Thousands lost their jobs."
(Related, but connection unstated)

**Clear causation**:
"The market crashed, and thousands lost their jobs as a result."
or
"Because the market crashed, thousands lost their jobs."
or
"The market crashed. Within weeks, thousands were unemployed."
(Temporal proximity implies causation)

### Causal Chains

For complex causation, build the chain:

"The drought killed the crops [cause 1]. Without crops, farmers had no income [effect 1/cause 2]. Without income, they couldn't pay mortgages [effect 2/cause 3]. Banks foreclosed [effect 3/cause 4]. Towns emptied [final effect]."

### Implicit vs. Explicit Causation

**Explicit**: Use connectives (because, therefore, so)
**Implicit**: Let proximity and logic imply connection

**Rules**: Be more explicit when:
- Causation is complex or unexpected
- Readers might miss the connection
- Precision matters (technical, legal, medical writing)

**Rules**: Be more implicit when:
- Causation is obvious
- You want faster pacing
- You trust your readers

## Cohesion Problems and Fixes

### Problem: Choppy Prose

**Symptoms**: Sentences feel disconnected, like a list.

**Diagnosis**: Lack of connectives, no referential chains, abrupt topic shifts.

**Fixes**:
1. Add transitional words
2. Build pronoun chains
3. Use given-new structure
4. Add bridging sentences

**Before**:
"The car stopped. A man got out. He walked to the door. The door opened."

**After**:
"The car stopped, and a man got out. He walked to the door. It opened before he could knock."

### Problem: Over-Connected Prose

**Symptoms**: Feels mechanical, like a high school essay.

**Diagnosis**: Too many connectives, obvious transitions, over-signaling.

**Fixes**:
1. Cut unnecessary connectives
2. Trust reader to infer connections
3. Vary connection types
4. Let some sentences stand alone

**Before**:
"First, she woke up. Then, she got dressed. After that, she ate breakfast. Subsequently, she left for work. Finally, she arrived."

**After**:
"She woke, dressed, ate breakfast standing at the counter. By eight she was at work."

### Problem: Lost Reference

**Symptoms**: Reader loses track of who or what is being discussed.

**Diagnosis**: Pronouns with ambiguous antecedents, too many referents, unclear shifts.

**Fixes**:
1. Use more specific noun phrases
2. Reduce number of active referents per paragraph
3. Re-establish reference after complex passages
4. Use names/clear identifiers at paragraph starts

### Problem: Topic Drift

**Symptoms**: Paragraph seems to wander, losing focus.

**Diagnosis**: Topic string breaks, sentences don't connect to main subject.

**Fixes**:
1. Identify the paragraph's core topic
2. Cut sentences that don't serve it
3. Reorder to maintain topic as subject
4. Add transition if topic legitimately shifts

## Cohesion Checklist

For any paragraph, verify:

- [ ] Clear topic in first 1-2 sentences
- [ ] Topic maintained or deliberately transitioned
- [ ] Pronouns have unambiguous antecedents
- [ ] Given-new flow in most sentences
- [ ] Appropriate connective density for genre/purpose
- [ ] Causal relationships clear (explicit or inferable)
- [ ] Final sentence provides closure or transition

For any passage (multiple paragraphs):

- [ ] Paragraphs link to each other (echo, hook, or bridge)
- [ ] Major topic shifts are signaled
- [ ] Referential chains track across paragraphs
- [ ] Overall argument/narrative thread is traceable
